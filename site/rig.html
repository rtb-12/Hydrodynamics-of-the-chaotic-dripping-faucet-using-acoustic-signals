The proposed rig

Two 12 by 12 inch tanks of 6 inch depth on a stand with adjustable bars, with two changes that the literature does not have. Both tanks hold a constant level by overflow, so neither the flow rate nor the fall height drifts during a run.

Why the overflows

The header tank

A 12 by 12 by 6 inch tank holds about 14 litres. Drained through a tap it obeys Torricelli’s law, so the outflow scales with √h and the drip rate falls continuously through every run.

That matters here more than in almost any other experiment, because flow rate is the bifurcation parameter. A faucet period-doubles as flow increases. If the flow drifts while you record, you are not sampling one point in the bifurcation diagram, you are smearing across it, and the return map turns to mush. This is why Roseberry’s runs were not repeatable and why Sekatchev had to detrend every 200-drop block.

An overflow standpipe at a fixed height fixes it without electronics. A small pump lifts more water than the nozzle passes, and the excess spills back down the standpipe. The level sits at the weir lip regardless of how the pump behaves, so head is set by geometry rather than by control.

The impact tank

This is the half that is easy to miss. Without its own overflow the lower tank fills as drops accumulate, and the water surface climbs toward the nozzle.

Work the numbers. A 12 by 12 inch footprint is 929 cm². At a drip rate of order 30 ml/min the tank gains 1800 cm³ per hour, so the level rises 1.9 cm every hour. Against the 86 mm fall height Phillips used, that is a 20-odd percent drift in fall distance, and therefore in impact velocity, over a single hour.

Move these numbers yourself in the lab. Impact velocity is what decides whether a bubble is entrained at all. Left alone, the rig slowly walks itself out of the acoustically audible window mid-run, and the microphone starts missing drops for a reason that has nothing to do with the dynamics being studied. A second overflow at a fixed level holds both the fall height and the tank acoustics constant indefinitely.

Why the loop returns to a separate sump

The pump loop must not discharge into the impact tank. A returning stream splashes, and splashing entrains air, which is precisely the sound the hydrophone is there to measure. Routing both overflows into a separate floor sump keeps the pump acoustically outside the experiment. The return outlet sits below the sump water line for the same reason.

The bonus: one rig, two experiments

Switch the pump off and the header stops being a constant-head device. It becomes a draining tank, and the rig turns into Sekatchev’s passive sweep: a slow monotonic descent through the whole bifurcation diagram in a single long run. Pump on for stationary runs at a fixed operating point, pump off for the sweep. The same hardware does both, so you are not choosing between the two philosophies in the literature.

Choosing the hydrophone

Almost every number needed to specify this is already in the papers. Working it through gives an answer that is cheaper than expected in one respect and stricter than expected in another.

What sets the bandwidth

The bubble rings at its Minnaert frequency, which depends only on its radius, and bubble radius follows drop size. Across the 1 to 5 mm entrainment window:

Drop diameterEntrained bubbleMinnaert frequencySource
5.0 mm0.887 mm7.4 kHzupper edge of the entrainment window
4.0 mm0.710 mm9.3 kHzPhillips measured 8.66 kHz for this bubble
3.0 mm0.532 mm12.3 kHzPhillips’ five tests spanned 7.90–12.23 kHz
2.0 mm0.355 mm18.5 kHzpast the top of most audio hardware
1.0 mm0.177 mm37.0 kHzlower edge of the entrainment window

Two caveats on that table. The bubble-to-drop ratio is extrapolated from Phillips’ single measured pair, and Phillips themselves found Minnaert overpredicts for small bubbles: their tests 4 and 5 recorded 11.72 and 12.23 kHz where Minnaert said 15.11 and 16.97. They could not explain it. So treat the 37 kHz figure as an upper bound with real uncertainty.

Above that sits Chashechkin’s territory. He recorded discrete spectral components at 31.4 to 94.2 kHz from the decaying part of the impact signal, using a hydrophone specified 0.002 to 100 kHz. Those are not the bubble ring; they are the cavity resonances that vanish above 4.6 m/s impact velocity. If Phase 2 is to reach them, the bandwidth requirement roughly triples.

Bandwidth is the binding constraint, and it is the one cheap hydrophones fail

Most inexpensive hydrophones are built for bioacoustics and underwater recording, so their integrated preamps are deliberately rolled off around 20 kHz. That covers a 3 mm drop and nothing smaller. Check the preamp bandwidth, not just the element’s.

What sets the sensitivity, and why it barely matters

Phillips recorded underwater peaks of about 40 Pa. In hydrophone units that is 152 dB re 1 µPa, which is a loud signal. Chashechkin’s range of 2.8 to 34.4 Pa agrees. What that means for a purchase:

SensitivityOutput from a 40 Pa plinkVerdict
−150 dB re 1V/µPa1.26 VHot. Chashechkin’s 30 mV/Pa unit is here. Watch for clipping
−165 dB re 1V/µPa224 mVIdeal. Line level, straight into an interface
−180 dB re 1V/µPa40 mVFine. Mic-level, use the interface’s preamp
−211 dB re 1V/µPa1.1 mVResearch grade, and the wrong tool. Needs a dedicated charge amp

This is the money-saving conclusion. Calibrated research hydrophones like the Reson TC4013 or Brüel & Kjær 8103 class are built for faint distant signals, so they sit near −211 dB and need matching charge amplifiers that cost more than the hydrophone. Your source is centimetres away and loud. A mid-sensitivity unit with an integrated preamp is both cheaper and easier to use.

Element size and placement

For an omnidirectional response the element must be small compared with the wavelength. Sound travels at about 1500 m/s in water, so at 40 kHz the wavelength is 37.5 mm and the element should be under about 12 mm. At 100 kHz the wavelength is 15 mm and the element needs to be under about 5 mm.

Placement follows Vazquez, who put his hydrophone at 5 cm from the source and says why: close enough for signal, far enough not to perturb the bubble formation. He explicitly criticises a 1 cm placement in an earlier paper for possibly influencing the physics. In a 12 inch tank, 5 cm from the impact point is comfortable.

The reverberation calculation that decides whether you must line the tank

With the hydrophone 5 cm from the impact in a 12 inch tank, the direct path is 5 cm and the first side-wall reflection travels about 31 cm. The extra 26 cm at 1500 m/s means the echo arrives 0.175 ms after the direct sound. Phillips’ wave packet lasts about 2 ms, so the first reflection lands roughly 9% of the way into it and everything after that is contaminated.

This splits cleanly by what you are measuring. Onset timing is immune, because the direct sound always arrives first and that is the only part the clock needs. Any measurement of decay rate, damping, or spectral shape is not, which is exactly why Phillips lined their tank with 11 mm MDF and found the bare tank made the packet grow before it decayed. Line the tank for Phase 2; do not bother for Phase 1.

Traps

  • Preamp roll-off. The element may be specified to 100 kHz while the built-in preamp is not. This is the single most common mismatch for this application.
  • Power type. Integrated preamps want either plug-in power, a few volts on a 3.5 mm jack, or 48 V phantom on XLR. Feeding phantom to a plug-in-power unit can destroy it. Match the hydrophone to the interface before ordering either.
  • Buying research grade. A −211 dB calibrated hydrophone is worse here than a −170 dB one, because it needs a charge amplifier you would also have to buy. Sensitivity is not the axis to optimise.
  • Sample rate you cannot use. A 100 kHz hydrophone into a 48 kHz interface throws away everything above 24 kHz. The interface must be bought to match.
  • Anti-alias filtering. Chashechkin’s own spectra run to 130 kHz, past his stated 100 kHz sensor limit, with no comment on roll-off. Know where your chain stops responding before trusting a spectral tail.

What this comes to

For Phase 1, an integrated-preamp hydrophone in the −165 to −180 dB range with honest response to 20 kHz, into a 96 kHz interface. The Aquarian H2a family is the usual low-cost choice at roughly ₹20–35 k; verify the specific variant’s preamp bandwidth against the table above rather than trusting the headline element figure.

For Phase 2, the requirement genuinely changes: flat to 100 kHz, element under 5 mm, and a 192 kHz or faster interface. That is a different and more expensive class of instrument, and it is worth deferring until Phase 1 has shown the acoustic clock works.

Build order

Ordered so that each step is testable before the next one depends on it.

  1. Frame and tanks, dry. Confirm the bars adjust smoothly and the fall height is readable to a millimetre.
  2. Plumbing and both overflows, water only, no nozzle. Run the pump for an hour. Both levels must sit dead still at their weir lips. This is the whole design in one test: if the levels move, nothing downstream is worth doing.
  3. Needle valve and nozzle. Confirm a stable drip and measure the range of achievable rates by catching drops in the graduated cylinder.
  4. Drop-volume calibration. Build Vd(T) across at least eight valve settings, following Sekatchev’s method, so drops per minute can be converted to millilitres per minute.
  5. Optical gate. Verify against a stopwatch and a hand count.
  6. Microphone and recording. Confirm the plink is audible above the room floor, and that its spectrum shows a clear peak.
  7. Cross-validation run. Mic and gate simultaneously, several thousand drops. Count agreement is the gate condition on the entire project.

Protocol

Phase 1: is the acoustic clock trustworthy?

Record the microphone and the optical gate at the same time and compare drop counts. The acceptance criterion is not "close enough". Phillips showed that drops outside roughly 1 to 5 mm, or outside the right impact-velocity window, entrain no bubble and are acoustically silent. One missed drop merges two intervals into one and injects a false point into the return map, which is worse than timing jitter because it adds structure that is not there rather than blurring structure that is.

So the first result of this project is a map of where in the operating range the acoustic clock is complete. That is a publishable result on its own and it is a prerequisite for everything after it.

Phase 1: the bifurcation diagram

With the clock validated, work at fixed operating points. At each valve setting record for long enough, and record the direction you approached the setting from. Two constraints from the literature set the run length and the protocol:

  • Run length. D’Innocenzo found chaotic transients lasting 250, 800 and 2200 drops before collapsing to a periodic rhythm. A 230-drop record like Roseberry’s can therefore classify a regime completely wrongly. Plan for several thousand drops per operating point.
  • Ramp direction. Ambravaneswaran reports hysteresis, and Coullet reproduces it: at the same nominal flow rate the faucet drips period-1 on an increasing ramp and period-2 on a decreasing one. Record ramp direction with every run or the data will not reconcile.

Then switch the pump off for a passive sweep and compare the two against each other. Nobody in the literature has done both on one rig.

Phase 2: what the sound itself carries

Add the hydrophone and, if a camera is available, video. Here the target is whether the waveform carries more than the arrival time. Coullet gives a specific reason to hope so: the ejected volume fraction Ve/V falls roughly linearly with flow rate, so drop size should co-vary with the control parameter, and drop size sets the entrained bubble radius, which sets the Minnaert frequency. If that chain holds, the pitch of each plink is a second observable alongside its timing.

The last row deserves attention before any money is spent, and the nozzle designer settles it in a few seconds. Ambravaneswaran found no chaotic regime at all for a fluid at Ohnesorge number 0.1, and chaos only at 0.01. Since Oh depends on the capillary radius and the fluid properties, the nozzle choice decides whether there is a cascade to listen to. Compute it first.
