The measurement

The plink is a bubble ringing down.

Block the bubble with a 0.71 mm rod, or drop the surface tension with a splash of washing-up liquid, and a falling drop hits water in total silence. Phillips, Agarwal and Jordan established this in 2018. What the plink actually is, is the shape below: a trapped air bubble oscillating at its natural frequency and dying away.

Hydrophone, underwater Microphone, airborne f ≈ 8.66 kHz · bubble ø 0.71 mm

The two traces carry the same frequency and the same envelope. That is the 2018 result: the airborne plink you hear is not the underwater sound leaking through the surface, it is the bubble driving the water surface directly. For this project the important part is narrower. The ring starts at a sharply defined instant, and that instant is when the bubble pinches off. It is an event you can put a timestamp on.

The gap

Two literatures that never met

The papers collected for this project fall into two groups, and the split is unusually clean.

The dripping faucet chaos work detects drops optically and contains no acoustics at all. Sekatchev used a phototransistor into a Raspberry Pi. Roseberry used a helium-neon laser and a photodiode. D’Innocenzo and Renna, Coullet, Mahadevan and Riera, and Ambravaneswaran, Phillips and Basaran are theory and simulation with no microphone in sight.

The drop impact acoustics work studies isolated single drops and contains no nonlinear dynamics. Phillips et al. recorded hundreds of impacts but never a bifurcation diagram, a return map, or a Lyapunov exponent. Chashechkin characterised two modes of sound emission from single drops. Vazquez et al. did apply chaos tooling to acoustic signals, but to a submerged bubbling nozzle rather than a dripping faucet.

Nobody has used the plink as the event clock for a period-doubling cascade. That is what this project is for.

Why the acoustic clock is worth building

Sekatchev reports a 0.025 s resolution floor on his phototransistor, and states plainly that it caused missed drops across 1.17 × 106 recorded events. A microphone sampling at 44.1 kHz timestamps an event to roughly 23 µs. That is three orders of magnitude sharper than the instrument the chaos literature has been relying on.

The catch

A microphone can miss a drop

Bubble entrainment only happens for drops of roughly 1 to 5 mm inside a specific impact-velocity window. Outside it, the impact is acoustically silent. An optical gate sees every drop; a microphone does not.

This matters more than timing jitter does. A missed drop merges two consecutive intervals into one long interval, which injects a false point into the return map rather than merely blurring a true one. So the first experiment is not the bifurcation diagram. It is running a microphone and an optical gate side by side and proving the acoustic clock catches every drop inside the operating window.

Current focus

Building the rig

Before any of that, the apparatus has to exist. The experimental setups page draws every rig in the literature in one shared visual language, so a syringe pump, a needle valve and a draining tank can be compared on sight, and lists every component the papers actually named with its specifications.

The proposed rig then works up the design for this project, and the one modification that matters: both tanks hold a constant level by overflow, so neither the flow rate nor the fall height drifts during a run. The theory page collects the mathematics behind all of it.
