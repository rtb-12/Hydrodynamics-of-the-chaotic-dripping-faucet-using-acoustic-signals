How the overflows actually work

Both constant-level devices are the same trick with no moving parts and no electronics: cut a hole, fit a bulkhead, and let the water decide its own level by spilling.

Two ways to pin a water level

cross-sections

Why the header uses a standpipe rather than a side hole: a standpipe is adjustable. Unscrew it, cut it shorter, and you have changed the head, which changes the pressure driving the needle valve. A side hole fixes the level permanently at whatever height you drilled.

The one sizing rule: the pump must deliver considerably more than the nozzle passes, so the standpipe is always spilling. If the pump ever falls below the drip rate the level drops off the rim and the whole guarantee evaporates. A 300 L/h pump against a 30 ml/min drip is a factor of 170, which is plenty of margin.

The optical gate circuit

An IR beam across the drop path, and a phototransistor watching it. When a drop passes, the beam dims and the voltage dips.

Photointerrupter into an audio input

five components
Why analogue straight in beats a digital pulse

A comparator would give a uniform clean pulse, which is tidier. But the raw dip carries extra information for free: its depth scales with how much of the beam the drop blocked, and its width with how long the drop took to cross. That is an independent estimate of drop size and speed on a channel you were recording anyway.

Add the comparator only if the raw signal-to-noise turns out poor. Start simple.

The aperture is what makes this work. A bare 5 mm LED throws a wide cone, so a 4 mm drop only dims it slightly and the edge is soft. Slide a short piece of black tube over both the LED and the phototransistor with a 1 to 2 mm hole, and the drop now blocks most of the beam, giving a deep sharp dip. This costs nothing and makes the difference between a usable gate and a marginal one.

Placement: a few centimetres below the nozzle, well above the water so splash never reaches it. It sees the drop about 4 ms after detachment, which is a constant offset that cancels in intervals.

Record all four to one file. That is the whole synchronisation strategy, and it is why the signal chain page insists on a four-channel interface rather than a cheaper two-channel one plus a separate data logger.

Grounding: the gate circuit and the interface must share a ground, and the pump must not. Run the pump from a separate socket and keep its cable away from the signal leads, or you will record mains hum at the drip rate.

The software

There is a working toolkit in analysis/. It uses only the Python standard library, so there is nothing to install: no numpy, no scipy, no build step.

Verify it before you trust it. This synthesises a recording with known drop times, runs the real detector over it, and checks the intervals come back:

cd analysis
python3 test_dripkit.py

Nineteen checks, about a second. Then processing a real run looks like this:

import dripkit as dk

ch, fs = dk.read_wav('run_042.wav')          # 4 channels, 96 kHz

acoustic = dk.detect(ch[0], fs)              # microphone, 4-20 kHz band
gate     = dk.detect(ch[2], fs, f_lo=200, f_hi=3000, k=5.0)

print(dk.match(acoustic, gate))              # <- the Phase 1 result
# {'n_gate': 1840, 'matched': 1836, 'missed': 4, 'false_pos': 0,
#  'detection_rate': 0.998, 'offset': 0.1325, 'jitter_us': 41.2}

T = dk.intervals(acoustic)
print('regime:', dk.classify(T))
dk.write_csv('run_042.csv', list(enumerate(T)), ['n', 'T_s'])

The three numbers match() returns are the deliverable of Phase 1. Detection rate says what fraction of real drops the microphone heard. False positives say whether it invented any. Jitter says how consistently the two channels agree on timing.

A bug the tests caught, worth knowing about

The first version estimated the channel offset from the median of each channel’s event times. That works perfectly when nothing is missing, and fails completely the moment the acoustic channel drops events, because the two medians then refer to different drops. It reported zero matches and 100% missed.

Which is to say it broke in exactly the situation the validation exists to measure, and would have looked fine on every clean test. It now estimates the offset from a histogram of pairwise gaps, which is robust to missing events. The test that plants deliberate silent drops is what found it.

Day-one checks

In order. Each one gates the next, so do not skip ahead.

  1. Frame and tanks, dry. Bars adjust smoothly, fall height readable to a millimetre.
  2. Water loop, no nozzle, one hour. Pump on, both overflows running. Mark both water lines with tape and come back in an hour. If either has moved, stop. Nothing downstream is worth doing until they sit still.
  3. Needle valve and nozzle. Get a stable drip. Measure the achievable range by catching drops in the cylinder.
  4. Drop-volume calibration. Eight or more valve settings, cylinder and stopwatch. This fixes your bifurcation diagram’s x-axis.
  5. Gate alone. Record 60 seconds, run dk.detect on it, and compare the count against drops you counted by ear. They must agree exactly.
  6. Microphone alone. Confirm the plink is clearly above the room floor and shows a spectral peak where the synthesiser says it should.
  7. Both together, several thousand drops. Run dk.match. This number is the gate condition on the whole project.

Step 2 is the one people skip and regret. It is a one-hour test with a roll of tape that validates the single design decision the rest of the rig depends on.
