Final results

What fall height, nozzle diameter and viscosity do to the impact sound

Each experiment changes one parameter of a single drop falling into a pool of the same liquid and records the airborne sound with a MEMS microphone. Every drop is measured against the room noise just before it, so levels are given as dB over room noise. Literature values come only from the papers in this project’s collection and are marked with their source.

1. Fall height

A 5 mm nozzle releases water drops of about 5 mm into plain water from 13 to 60 cm. The 15, 25 and 35 cm recordings are the earlier final run; 13, 33, 43 and 60 cm are new.

What we measured

Only at 13 cm, the height nearest the regular entrainment window, does every drop ring (21 of 21); from 15 to 60 cm the share falls to 35–80% with no steady trend. Ring pitch scatters between 2.3 and 6.2 kHz with no trend in height, and the level stays at 20–36 dB over room noise up to 43 cm before falling to 15 dB at 60 cm. Rings decay over 7–17.5 ms, inside the 3–19 ms that Chashechkin (2024) reports, but without the growth with impact speed he finds. The 43 cm rings sit close to a 2.15 kHz tone also heard between drops, so that pitch is the least certain.

Against the literature

For a 4.5 mm drop, regular bubble entrainment, one bubble and one clean ring per drop, needs U ≈ 1.10–1.42 m/s, about 6–10 cm of fall (Oguz & Prosperetti 1990 fit, as cited by Phillips et al. 2018; consistent with Pumphrey & Walton 1988, p. 227: a reproducible 7 kHz tone at 7–8 cm). Our heights give U = 1.6–3.3 m/s, so every condition lies above that window, in the splash regime where Chashechkin (2024, Table 4) records resonance packets only irregularly between 1.98 and 4.43 m/s and none above 4.6 m/s.

2. Nozzle diameter

Nozzles of 2, 5 and 7 mm internal diameter, water falling 25 cm into water. Tate’s law with a Harkins–Brown factor of 0.60–0.72 predicts drops of about 3.9, 5.2 and 5.9 mm; the 4 mm row of the same calculation reproduces the 5.0 mm drop that Chashechkin & Prokhorov (2014, p. 3) measure from a 4 mm nozzle.

What we measured

The 2 mm nozzle’s drops are about 9 dB quieter than the 5 and 7 mm drops, which sound alike; with only 9 drops at 2 mm this is the weakest result on the page. Half to four fifths of the drops ring at every nozzle size.

Against the literature

Drop mass grows only linearly with nozzle radius, so drop diameter changes by about 1.5× across 2–7 mm and large acoustic differences are not expected from drop size alone. At 25 cm (U = 2.2 m/s) none of the three drops is inside its regular entrainment window, and Phillips et al. (2018, p. 2) report no regular entrainment for drops above 5 mm, which includes the 7 mm nozzle’s drop.

3. Viscosity

The same 5 mm nozzle and 25 cm fall with water, a 50% glycerin–water mixture and pure glycerin, each falling into a pool of the same liquid. Both glycerin runs were also filmed with sound; the films below play their own soundtrack, recorded with the picture, so each splash and its click arrive together. The films are separate takes from the measured recordings: their drips run at a different rate, so neither can be lined up with a recording, and the numbers come from the recordings alone.

Liquid properties are textbook values at 20 °C; We and Re take the drop sizes from Tate’s law (5.1, 4.9 and 4.5 mm).

What we measured

50% glycerin (Oh ≈ 0.01) sounds much like water: 34 against 30 dB over room noise, with about half the drops ringing, but its rings die away twice as fast, 8.4 against 16.2 ms, as viscous damping predicts. Pure glycerin (Oh ≈ 2.4) stays at the room noise and none of its 13 drops rings: the impact is silent.

Against the literature

No paper in the collection measures drop-impact sound in any liquid but water. The physics points one way: the viscous damping constant of a 0.4 mm bubble is about 0.0005 in water but about 0.6 in glycerin, so a glycerin bubble rings for about one cycle, and Re ≈ 9 leaves little inertia to fold a cavity shut. Phillips et al. (2018, p. 6) silence the impact by blocking entrainment with a rod or a surfactant; glycerin is a third way to lose the bubble and with it the sound.

4. Drop formation and impact, high-speed video

Backlit shadowgraph clips from a Phantom Miro C110 at 1280 × 1024: the drop arrives, opens a crater, and the crater’s collapse throws up a Worthington jet that pinches off a droplet. The clips were cleaned of the camera software’s tracking marks, denoised and upscaled 2× with every frame kept. Step frame by frame, or click a still to jump to that stage. Every clip comes from the same 5 mm nozzle, so the impacting drop has one diameter D throughout, and D measured in each clip is the ruler that puts the three on one scale: the bar on each video is 1 D. Times assume 900 fps, the camera’s rate at full resolution, which the files do not record. Fall speed in D per frame checks it only to 5–10%: the 35 cm to 25 cm speed ratio comes out 1.05–1.17 against 1.18 expected, and a 5.0 mm drop would imply 940–1070 fps; lengths in D do not depend on it. Both 25 cm jets leave the frame, so their starred stills mark the middle of the time off frame, not the apex.

Against the literature

In the Pumphrey–Phillips mechanism a single bubble pinches off the crater bottom about 24–27 ms after contact and rings at once. In the splash regime of 5 mm drops the crater bottoms out near 23 ms, the jet rises near 140 ms and the sounding gas cavities form at 170–230 ms (Chashechkin & Prokhorov 2025, Prokhorov & Chashechkin 2011). In all three clips the crater reaches its deepest point 21–22 frames after contact, 23–24 ms at 900 fps, matching the 23 ms crater bottom of Chashechkin & Prokhorov (2025) for a 5 mm drop at 2.4 m/s. The jet then rises and pinches off its top droplet 130–150 ms after contact, the splash-regime sequence rather than a single bubble at the crater bottom. Scaled by D, the crater is about 2.3 D deep at both heights, but the jets differ: both 25 cm jets are thin, rise at 0.22–0.26 D/ms and leave the frame, while the 35 cm jet is thicker, rises at 0.14 D/ms, tops out at 6.0 D and pinches off a droplet of 1.2 D instead of 0.7 D. No paper in the collection measures jet height, so this is an observation from three clips, not a comparison.

Limitations

  • The microphone stores HE-AAC at 48 kHz, which synthesises the band above about 9 kHz, so every measure uses 2–9 kHz and rings above 9 kHz are not seen.
  • The recorder applies its own noise suppression and gain control; dB over room noise cancels slow gain changes but is not a sound pressure level.
  • One recording per condition, of about one minute; spreads are the middle half of the drops in that recording, not run-to-run repeatability.
  • Drop diameters are predicted from Tate’s law, not measured.
