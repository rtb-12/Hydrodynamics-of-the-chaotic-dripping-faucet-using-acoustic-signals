Instrumentation

Data collection and analysis

Use all three sensors. They are not redundant, they play different roles, and the whole design hinges on one decision: put every one of them on the same clock.

The three channels, and what each is for

ChannelMeasuresIts unique jobIts weakness
Optical gate drop breaks a light beam Ground truth. It sees every drop including the silent ones. Nothing else can validate the acoustic clock Tells you nothing about the drop. Needs physical beam access, so it can never be deployed outside a lab
Surface microphone airborne pressure, ~0.3 Pa The deployable channel. Exactly what a real leak detector or rain gauge would use About 40 dB weaker than underwater, and competing with room noise
Hydrophone underwater pressure, ~40 Pa The reference channel. Best signal-to-noise, cleanest spectrum for reading bubble size Sits in the tank, so it must be far enough out not to perturb the impact. Vazquez used 5 cm
The framing that makes this coherent

The optical gate is your calibration instrument, not your measurement. Its entire job is to prove the acoustic clock misses nothing. Once proven, the science runs on sound, because sound carries information the gate never can, and because sound is what you could actually point at a real dripping tap.

Why keep both acoustic channels rather than picking one? Because Phillips showed they carry the same frequency and the same envelope, with a cross-correlation peak of 0.777. Recording both lets you measure the roughly 40 dB interface attenuation yourself and check it against their result, which is a free validation of your whole acoustic chain. Then the hydrophone gives you the physics at the best signal-to-noise, and the microphone proves it still works in the configuration a real application would have to use.

One drop’s life across the sensors

The channels do not see the same instant, and knowing the offsets is what lets you match them up.

TimeEventWhich channel sees it
0 msDrop detaches from the nozzlenone directly, yet this is the event the chaos is about
~4 msDrop crosses the gate beam a few cm downoptical, sharp edge
132 msDrop hits the water, from 86 mma weak initial contact pulse on both acoustic channels
+ a few msCrater forms, recoils, closessilent
→Bubble pinches offmic + hydrophone, the strong wave packet
+ ~2 msRing decays awayboth, then silence
+ tens of msJet rises, droplets fly, ripples spreadsilent, all of it

The gate and the acoustics are therefore separated by one fall time, about 132 ms at 86 mm. That is a constant offset, not a distortion, so intervals survive it intact.

Worth checking rather than assuming: does a bigger drop fall faster and smear that offset? At 86 mm you only reach about 14% of terminal velocity, so drag costs roughly 0.5% of impact speed and the size dependence of fall time is under 0.1 ms. Phillips’ full terminal-velocity treatment gives 1.29 m/s where naive free fall gives 1.299. The offset holds.

The signal chain

Everything on one clock

4 channels · 96 kHz · 24 bit
The highest-leverage decision in the build, and it is free

Do not put the optical gate on its own DAQ. Feed the photodetector, through a comparator and an attenuator, into the fourth channel of the same interface as the microphone and hydrophone.

Now every event on every channel is on one timeline to the sample. No drift over a six-hour run, no alignment code, no sync protocol. Chashechkin did the equivalent with a four-channel 10 MHz interface, and it is why his frames and pressure traces share a time base.

Two practical notes. Audio inputs are AC-coupled, so a steady voltage level will not pass, but an edge will: make the gate circuit emit a short pulse per drop rather than a level, and it records cleanly. And check the levels, since a 5 V logic pulse will clip a line input that expects about 1 V, so attenuate.

What to actually store

Raw four-channel audio at 96 kHz and 24 bit is 1.15 MB/s, which is 4.1 GB per hour. For a short validation run that is fine. For anything like Sekatchev’s 2.5 days it is hopeless.

So detect events live and keep only what carries information: the event time, plus a short waveform snippet around each drop.

StrategyPer drop1 hour, ~5 drops/s1.17 million drops
Raw continuous—4.1 GB~250 GB, impractical
Event times + 5 ms snippets5.8 kB105 MB6.7 GB
Event times only~30 B0.5 MB35 MB

Keep the snippets. Five milliseconds at 96 kHz is 480 samples, comfortably more than the 2 ms the wave packet lasts, and it is what lets you go back later and extract the spectral peak per drop without re-running the experiment. Storing only event times throws away the entire acoustic half of the project.

Six analysis stages

From waveform to bifurcation diagram

each stage feeds the next

1. Onsets

Bandpass the acoustic channels around the expected bubble band, say 5 to 20 kHz, which kills room rumble and pump hum without touching the signal. Take the envelope, threshold it, and apply a minimum-interval debounce. Roseberry did not debounce and got a spurious spike of zero-length intervals she had to explain away as an artefact; do not repeat that.

The gate channel is easier: detect the edge.

2. Validation. This is the whole of Phase 1

Match each acoustic event to a gate event within a window, allowing for the constant fall delay. Then report three numbers: detection rate (what fraction of real drops the microphone heard), false positive rate, and timing scatter between the two channels.

This is not a preliminary check to skim past. Phillips showed drops outside roughly 1 to 5 mm, or outside the right velocity window, entrain no bubble and land silently. A missed drop merges two intervals into one and injects a point into the return map that corresponds to nothing. That does not blur your result, it fabricates structure. Mapping where in the operating range the acoustic clock is complete is a publishable result on its own.

3. Intervals

Tn = tn − tn−1. Everything downstream is this one list of numbers.

4. Calibrate the control parameter

Catch drops in a graduated cylinder at eight or more valve settings and fit drop volume against period, following Sekatchev. Without this your bifurcation diagram has drops per minute on the horizontal axis instead of millilitres per minute, and drop volume depends non-linearly on flow rate, so the axis is distorted and cannot be compared to anybody’s simulation. It costs an afternoon.

5. Dynamics

Now the standard toolkit, in increasing order of ambition. Time series of Tn against index, which is where you spot transients and drift. Return map. Histogram. Bifurcation diagram, both from stationary operating points and from a passive sweep. Delay embedding and correlation dimension. And then the Lyapunov exponent, which not one of the eight papers computes, including the ones that use the word chaos throughout.

Two constraints from the literature govern how you run this. D’Innocenzo found chaotic transients lasting up to 2200 drops, so plan thousands of drops per operating point. Ambravaneswaran found hysteresis, so record which direction you approached each flow rate from or the data will not reconcile.

6. Per-drop spectra. The new part

Take each stored snippet, find its spectral peak, and invert Minnaert to get the bubble radius. Now you have a second sequence running alongside Tn: a sequence of pitches.

The question nobody has asked

You will have two sequences from the same drops: when each one landed, and what note it made. So:

Does the pitch sequence period-double at the same flow rates the timing sequence does?

If yes, you have a second independent observable for the same attractor, and an acoustic sensor becomes strictly more informative than every optical drop counter in the literature.

If no, that is Vazquez’s warning coming true, and it is just as interesting.

The reason to expect yes: Coullet showed the ejected volume fraction falls roughly linearly with flow rate, so drop size co-varies with the control parameter, and drop size sets the bubble, and the bubble sets the pitch. Every link in that chain is established. Nobody has traversed the whole thing.

The reason to expect no: Vazquez recorded acoustic and pressure signals from the same bubbling flow and found they went chaotic in different regimes, because they report different state variables on timescales two decades apart. His acoustic channel measured bubble size while his pressure channel measured production rate, and the two did not agree.

Either answer is a result. That is the mark of a well-posed question, and it is where this project stops being a replication and starts being research.

One clock Four channels, one converter, no synchronisation code. This is the decision to get right on day one.
