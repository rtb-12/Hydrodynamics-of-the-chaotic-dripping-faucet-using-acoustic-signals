Underlying concepts

Theory

Four separate model families describe this system, built by people who were not talking to each other. Three describe why a faucet drips chaotically. One describes why a drop makes a noise. Reading them side by side is how you find where they should join.

The observable

Every chaos paper in the collection reduces the faucet to a single number sequence: the time between consecutive drops.

Inter-drop interval Tn = tn − tn−1
tn
absolute time of the n-th drop event, in seconds
Tn
the n-th interval. This is the entire dynamical payload

This is why acoustics can substitute for optics at all. Whether the arrival time comes from a broken laser beam or from the onset of a plink, the downstream analysis is identical.

Ambravaneswaran et al. push back on the whole approach, and the criticism applies to a microphone exactly as it applies to a drop counter. Their words: such studies “neglect entirely the physics of drop formation and view the system as a black box that generates a stream of numbers.” The interval sequence is only an approximate Poincaré section of the true state space. Their answer was to measure the drop shape instead, specifically the limiting length Ld at breakup. That is a fair warning to carry: sound, like a laser gate, gives events rather than shapes.

Dimensionless groups, and whether chaos exists at all

Ambravaneswaran non-dimensionalise lengths by the capillary radius R and times by the inertio-capillary time, which leaves three groups.

Capillary time, Ohnesorge, Bond, Weber τc = √(ρR³ / σ)
Oh = μ / √(ρRσ)
G = ρgR² / σ
We = ρUf²R / σ   with  Q = πR²Uf
ρ, σ, μ
density, surface tension, dynamic viscosity of the liquid
R
capillary radius, the length scale
Uf
mean liquid velocity in the capillary
Q
volumetric flow rate, the laboratory knob
Oh
viscous forces against inertia and capillarity. Fixed by the fluid and the nozzle
G

We

Check this before buying a nozzle

Ambravaneswaran found no chaotic regime at all for their system S at Oh = 0.1. Chaos appeared only when they dropped to Oh = 0.01. They also report that at Oh of order 1 the faucet goes straight from period-1 to a jet with no cascade at all.

So viscosity and capillary radius, not just flow rate, decide whether there is anything to listen to. This is a design constraint on the rig, not a footnote.

Worked for water at 20 °C, taking ρ = 998 kg/m³, σ = 0.0728 N/m, μ = 1.00×10−3 Pa·s:

Run these numbers for your own nozzle in the lab.

The encouraging result is that plain water sits at Oh of a few thousandths across every plausible nozzle, which is on the chaotic side of the Oh = 0.01 case rather than the chaos-free Oh = 0.1 case. A capillary near R = 2 mm additionally puts G near 0.5, which is exactly the system whose bifurcation diagram Ambravaneswaran mapped zone by zone. These are estimates from tabulated fluid properties, not measurements, and they assume R is the inner radius.

Family 1: mass on a spring

The oldest and simplest picture, from Shaw through Martien to D’Innocenzo and Renna. A growing drop is a point mass on a damped spring, where the spring stands in for surface tension and the damping for viscous friction at the nozzle.

D’Innocenzo & Renna, Eqs. 1–2 d(Mv)/dt = Mg − kx − bv    dM/dt = R

expanding the product rule:
M dv/dt = Mg − kx − (R + b)v
M
instantaneous mass of the hanging drop, g
x, v
downward position and speed of its centre of mass
k
spring constant standing in for surface tension, dynes/cm. About 475 for water
b
damping, g/s, absorbing viscosity and nozzle geometry
R
flow rate, ml/s. The control parameter

Note what the product rule does: the inflow itself acts as extra damping, so the effective friction is (R + b) and not b. The control parameter enters the dynamics, not just the mass bookkeeping.

The smooth equation above is not where the chaos lives. It lives in the threshold rule applied when x reaches a critical distance xc.

Breakoff, the actual nonlinearity (i)  ΔM = α Mc vc    (momentum-proportional)
(ii) ΔM = α vc         (speed-proportional, Martien)

residue restarts at  x0 = xc − r·ΔM/Mc,  r = (3ΔM / 4πρ)1/3
Mc, vc
mass and speed at the instant of threshold
α
fitted breakoff coefficient
xc
critical displacement triggering breakoff. A second control parameter

This exact model runs live in the lab, at these parameters, with the flow rate on a slider.

D’Innocenzo and Renna’s central claim is that this rule, not the differential equation, produces the transition to chaos, and that the two candidate laws give genuinely different routes: period doubling from R ≈ 0.61 ml/s for law (i), versus a biperiodic route past R ≈ 0.825 ml/s for law (ii). They prefer (i) because (ii) makes drip intervals decrease as xc grows, which is physically wrong.

The result that sets your run length

The same paper found chaotic transients lasting 250, 800 and 2200 drops before collapsing to a periodic rhythm. Their own 50-point-per-flow-rate bifurcation diagrams could not see them. Any record shorter than a few thousand drops can misclassify a regime outright.

Ambravaneswaran are blunt about this whole family: “Common to these models is the ad hoc manner in which drop breakup is mimicked by the removal of a specified amount of mass from the existing mass when the spring extension exceeds a threshold.” The models reproduce the phenomenology with knobs that have no derivation from fluid mechanics.

Family 2: slender-jet hydrodynamics

Ambravaneswaran, Phillips and Basaran replace the spring with an actual one-dimensional reduction of the axisymmetric Navier-Stokes equations, solved by finite elements through hundreds of consecutive drops.

Slender-jet equations, dimensionless ∂vz/∂t = −vz ∂vz/∂z − ∂(2H)/∂z + 3Oh (1/h²) ∂/∂z(h² ∂vz/∂z) + G

∂h/∂t = −vz ∂h/∂z − ½ h ∂vz/∂z
h(z,t)
neck radius at distance z below the capillary exit
vz(z,t)
axial velocity of the liquid
2H
twice the mean curvature. Its gradient is the Laplace-pressure driving force
3 Oh
viscous extensional term. The 3 is the Trouton ratio for a Newtonian filament
G
gravity as a constant body force in these units

Pinch-off is declared when the minimum neck radius falls below 10−3; the drop is discarded, the mesh rebuilt, and integration continues. That discard-and-remesh loop is what makes long time series possible.

Two results from this family matter for the experiment. First, hysteresis, reported here for the first time in a dripping faucet: in one zone the same Weber number gives period-1 on an increasing sweep and period-2 on a decreasing one, and only one of the two can be observed at a time. Second, the observation that chaos is not guaranteed: their reference system at Oh = 0.1 period-doubles, halves back, jumps, and returns to period-1 before jetting at We ≳ 0.26, with no chaotic regime anywhere.

Family 3: two normal forms

Coullet, Mahadevan and Riera ask a different question. Not how to simulate the faucet accurately, but what the minimum dynamical content is that produces the observed chaos. Their answer is that only two modes matter.

Linearising the full Lagrangian lubrication model about its static pendant-drop solutions shows a clean spectral gap: an oscillatory damped mode, and a mode that reaches the origin exactly at the critical volume Vc. Everything else damps out roughly ten times faster. A proper orthogonal decomposition of the simulated drop shapes agrees, and time-delay embedding needs only three dimensions.

Reduced model: saddle-node plus damped oscillator ∂ttX + η(V) ∂tX = a(V) + b(V) X²
∂tV = q

with  a(V) = (V² − Vc²)/V,  b(V) = α/V³
X
projection of the drop shape onto a spatial mode. Not simply the centre of gravity
V, Vc
drop volume and the critical volume where the static solution is lost
q
imposed flow rate
a + bX²
exactly the normal form of a saddle-node bifurcation

The physical picture is a particle in a cubic potential whose local minimum, the stable pendant drop, annihilates with the saddle as the drop fills. Pinch-off is a global reinjection that resets the system. That combination has a name.

The mechanism, and the group that controls it dripping = saddle-node (Andronov) + Shilnikov homoclinic reinjection

τf ~ R / v0       (filling time)
f ~ √(Γ / ρV)     (recoil frequency of the residual drop)
1/τd ~ √(fη / V1/3) (damping rate of that recoil)

the decisive ratio: τf / τd
τf
time to refill the pendant drop to Vc
τd
time for the post-pinch-off ringing to die away

This ratio is the clearest mechanistic statement of why a faucet goes chaotic. If the residual drop has stopped ringing before it refills to Vc, every drop starts from the same state and dripping is periodic. If it is still ringing, then whether it reaches Vc early or late depends on the phase of that oscillation, so alternately a larger or smaller drop detaches, whose own ringing is damped sooner or later, and the memory propagates. Period doubling is that feedback closing on itself.

Family 4: where the sound comes from

Entirely separate literature, entirely separate people. Minnaert derived the natural frequency of a submerged gas bubble in 1933, and Phillips, Agarwal and Jordan confirmed in 2018 that this is what a dripping tap actually is.

Minnaert frequency f = (1 / 2πa) · √(3γP0 / ρ)
a
bubble radius, m. The only thing that really varies
γ
ratio of specific heats of the trapped gas
P0
static pressure at the bubble surface, Pa
ρ
density of the surrounding liquid

Hear it in the lab: move the bubble radius and listen to the pitch move. Since f ∝ 1/a, a smaller bubble rings higher. Phillips measured 8.66 kHz for a bubble of stabilised diameter 0.71 mm. Vazquez inverted the same relation to size bubbles from sound, reading 1500 to 4975 µm from a spectral peak that moved down as flow rate rose. The relation runs in both directions, which is what makes acoustic sizing possible at all.

What actually radiates is not the bubble's presence but its volume acceleration.

Radiated pressure, and the interface problem p ∝ d²V/dt²

|p′a| / |p′w| = ½ (ρa/ρw) (r0/d)² (R/z0)
V
volume enclosed by the pulsating bubble
p′a, p′w
airborne and underwater pressure perturbations
r0
radius of the cavity floor, acting as a piston
d
depth of the bubble below the cavity floor
z0, R
microphone and hydrophone distances from the source

The second equation is Phillips’ contribution and it settles an old argument. Simple transmission through the water surface predicts about 70 dB of attenuation. They measured about 40 dB. Their model, in which the bubble drives the cavity floor as a piston, predicts 44 dB. The (r0/d)² factor is the amplification that makes a plink audible across a room.

Two facts from this family constrain the experiment directly. The sound begins exactly at bubble pinch-off, not at impact, which is what makes it a usable clock. And blocking entrainment, whether with a 0.71 mm rod or with a drop of surfactant, gives a completely silent impact, which is what makes silent drops a real risk.

Analysis tooling

Return map and the Feigenbaum constants plot ( Tn , Tn+1 )   period-1 → one point on the diagonal
                              period-2 → two clusters

δ = lim (Fm−1 − Fm−2) / (Fm − Fm−1) = 4.669...
α = lim ΔTm / ΔTm+1 = 2.502...
Fm
flow rate at the m-th period doubling. Horizontal spacing
ΔTm
branch separation in a period-m regime. Vertical spacing

Sekatchev makes a point about these that is easy to miss and expensive to get wrong. Because δ is built from horizontal distances, it needs the true volumetric flow rate in ml/min, not drops per minute, and drop volume depends non-linearly on flow rate. So the axis needs a calibration:

Drop-volume calibration Vd(T) = A TB + C

then  F [ml/min] = Fd [drops/min] × Vd(T)
Vd
volume per drop, ml. Sekatchev measured 0.032 to 0.047 ml
A, B, C
fitted per nozzle. His values: −28×10−7, −2.8, 467×10−4

α survives a drops-per-minute axis because it only involves vertical distances. δ does not. Neither constant has ever been extracted from a real faucet, because nobody has obtained a clean enough period-2 to period-4 transition.

Attractor reconstruction vk = ( xk, xk−τ, ..., xk−(d−1)τ )

C(r) ~ rD2    so D2 = slope of ln C(r) against ln r
τ
time delay, in samples
d
embedding dimension. Coullet found 3 sufficient for this system
D2
correlation dimension. Vazquez measured 3.4 to 5.5 on acoustic data

Vazquez reports a practical failure worth knowing in advance: the standard average-mutual- information prescription for τ gave values at which the correlation dimension never saturated, and the delays that did work had to be found by sweeping. Their acoustic embedding also failed to converge in exactly the transitional regime, which is the interesting one.

The chain this project is betting on

Put the four families end to end and there is a causal chain from the tap handle to the waveform. Each link is established in the literature. No paper traverses more than two of them.

LinkMechanismEstablished by
Flow rate → regimeWe is the bifurcation parameter; period doubling, hysteresis, chaosAmbravaneswaran; Coullet; D’Innocenzo
Regime → interval sequenceTn is the low-dimensional observable, embedding dimension 3Coullet; Sekatchev
Flow rate → drop sizeEjected volume fraction Ve/V falls roughly linearly with flow rateCoullet, Fig. 10
Drop size → bubble sizeEntrainment geometry: cavity radius and depth scale with the dropPhillips
Bubble size → pitchMinnaert, f ∝ 1/aMinnaert 1933; Phillips; Vazquez
Pinch-off → onset timeSound starts exactly at bubble pinch-offPhillips, frame 8 at 30,000 fps

Read the last two rows together. If drop size co-varies with the control parameter, and bubble size follows drop size, and pitch follows bubble size, then each plink carries two independent numbers: when it happened, and what note it was. The timing gives the interval sequence that the whole chaos literature is built on. The pitch would be a second observable that no optical gate can provide at all.

That is the interesting hypothesis, and it is genuinely untested. Vazquez supplies the caution: in his bubbling flow the acoustic channel and the mechanical channel went chaotic in different regimes, because they report different state variables on timescales two decades apart. Pitch and timing may not agree, and if they disagree that is a result rather than a failure.
